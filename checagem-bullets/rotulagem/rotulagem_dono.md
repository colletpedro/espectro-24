# Rotulagem manual — o bullet tem apoio nesta review?

São 40 pares. Em cada um, um TEMA (o bullet da síntese, com a descrição que aparece em "Aprofundar") e UMA review do mesmo grupo. A pergunta é a mesma que o juiz vai responder, com o mesmo critério:

> **A review menciona este tema?**

- **sim**: a review fala do mesmo ASSUNTO do tema e com o mesmo JUÍZO (elogio, crítica ou observação na mesma direção). Basta uma frase da review, em qualquer idioma.
- **não**: a review não fala do assunto; ou fala do assunto com o juízo oposto; ou é só compatível com o tema, sem dizê-lo.
- A descrição serve só para entender o que o tema quer dizer. Ignore quantas pessoas ela diz que concordam: aqui se julga UMA review.

Responda em `rotulos_dono.csv` (mesma pasta), uma linha por par: `sim` ou `não`. Não deixe em branco — par sem rótulo trava a validação.

## P20 — Napoleão · quem gostou (notas 4 a 5)

**Tema:** Ritmo e edição

**Descrição:** A versão estendida foi elogiada por melhorar o ritmo, dando mais profundidade aos eventos, enquanto a versão original parecia correr por alguns marcos históricos.

**Review** (nota 4.0 de 5 estrelas):

> This cut is so much better than the original. While I was planning to save Ridley Scott’s Napoleon until I reached the next chapter of my director deep dive, the opportunity arose to watch it, so I took it. At three and a half hours, Scott’s director’s cut is a substantial expansion of the theatrical version. It doesn’t quite add the sense of scale I expected from an extra hour of runtime, but that’s easy to forgive when the film delivers on the level that it does. Napoleon certainly has its flaws, and some of them are significant, but in the grand scheme of things, it’s a great time. From the opening sequence, the film radiates a grit that seems to seep off the screen, steadily building throughout the runtime. That escalation culminates in several disturbing scenes of political violence that are incredibly effective. Scott captures the messiness of government, revolution, and monarchy with remarkable clarity, making it easy to understand the absolute chaos of France’s political climate during this period. The director’s cut is far more expansive and patient with its pacing, allowing these elements to breathe and making the atmosphere all the more effective. Much of that atmosphere is still rooted in the relationship between Josephine and Napoleon, just as it was in the original cut. What elevates it here are the expanded plotlines surrounding Napoleon’s rise to power, along with the greater focus given to key political and historical developments. These additions provide the film with far more depth and substance than its theatrical counterpart. My biggest issue with the film, both the director’s cut and the theatrical version, is its use of American accents in place of French ones. It’s a truly baffling decision that repeatedly pulls the viewer out of the film’s otherwise immersive atmosphere. It feels especially strange when you have a Russian character speaking with a Russian accent while a native Frenchman speaks with an American one. It constantly distracted me. I understand why Joaquin Phoenix was cast, he is excellent and perfectly suited to the role in many ways. However, I can’t understand why he doesn’t attempt a French accent at all. While his star power undoubtedly helped the film commercially, the decision to forgo an accent altogether may have avoided certain pitfalls, but it ultimately hurts the film’s authenticity and integrity.
